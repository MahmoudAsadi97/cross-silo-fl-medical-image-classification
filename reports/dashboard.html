The problem

Why hospitals can’t just pool their data

Hospitals hold exactly the data needed to train good diagnostic AI — but privacy law and ethics mean patient images usually cannot leave the building. So how do several hospitals train one strong model together without sharing any raw images?

Our answer in one line
Federated Learning (FL): each hospital trains on its own images and sends only the model updates (numbers) to a coordinator, which averages them into a shared model. The images never move.
Why it matters
This is the whole premise of the project: collaboration on sensitive medical data with privacy built in, on a real benchmark (Fed-ISIC2019 — ~23,000 skin-lesion photos, 8 diagnoses, 6 hospitals).
The data

The six hospitals are very different (“non-IID”)

Before training, we measured how different the hospitals’ data is. They are highly uneven: sizes range from 7,947 down to 281 images, and some hospitals are missing whole disease classes. This unevenness is what makes federated learning hard.

Why it matters
If every hospital had the same mix, averaging their models would be easy. Because they don’t, naive averaging can wobble — which motivates every method below.
How we measured it (for the curious)
We computed each hospital’s class distribution and its distance from the global mix (Jensen–Shannon divergence), plus counts of missing classes and label entropy. Hospital 1 is the most skewed (misses 2 classes).
Core result

Does federation actually work? Yes.

We compared training strategies fairly (same model, data, and budget — only the method changes). FedAvg reaches 0.32, sitting between training alone (0.21) and the ideal of pooling all data (0.46). It recovers ~45% of that gap without sharing images.

An honest finding we’re proud of
The fancier methods (FedProx, SCAFFOLD, and FedAdam which we also built) cut drift but did not beat plain FedAvg on this data. We report that straight: on this benchmark, well-tuned FedAvg is the strong baseline. Honesty > hype.
Why 0.32 and not higher?
“Balanced accuracy” is a strict metric on 8 imbalanced classes (random = 0.125). We also used a smaller model at lower resolution so it fits a laptop GPU and works with privacy. The point was measuring the trade-offs, not chasing a leaderboard.
Privacy — 1

Adding a mathematical privacy guarantee (Differential Privacy)

Model updates can still leak information. Differential Privacy (DP) adds calibrated noise so no single patient changes the result much. A knob, ε (epsilon), sets the strength: smaller ε = more privacy = lower accuracy.

What we added & why
We built our own privacy accountant from scratch (pure Python) to double-check the ε numbers, rather than trusting the library blindly — so the guarantee is verified.
Why it matters
It turns “we added noise” into a quantified promise: e.g. ε≈38 keeps accuracy at 0.21; very strong privacy (ε≈5) drops it to 0.15. Leaders can pick the point.
Privacy — 2

Proving the privacy works: a membership-inference attack

We didn’t just claim privacy — we attacked the model. A membership-inference attack tries to tell whether a specific patient was in the training set. Score 0.5 = the attacker is guessing; above 0.5 = leakage.

Why it matters
This is empirical proof, not a promise: DP measurably shuts down the attack (0.55→0.50). Attack-and-defend is exactly how security is demonstrated.
Privacy — 3

Hiding each hospital’s update from the coordinator (Secure Aggregation)

Even the coordinator shouldn’t see any one hospital’s update. With secure aggregation, hospitals add secret masks that cancel out when summed — the server learns only the total, never an individual contribution.

Why it matters
The coordinator gets the average it needs to improve the model, but cannot inspect any single hospital — closing the “curious server” risk. Verified exact to 13 decimal places.
Security — integrity

What if a hospital is malicious? (Poisoning & defense)

The privacy work protects confidentiality. But a malicious or hacked hospital could send poisoned updates to sabotage the shared model. We simulated exactly that (2 of 6 hospitals attacking) and tested defenses.

What we added & why
This is a brand-new dimension: a model-poisoning attack plus three robust aggregators (median, trimmed-mean, Krum) that limit how much any one hospital can move the model. It completes the security story: confidentiality and integrity.
Why it matters
A single defenseless average is wrecked by 2 bad actors (drops to 0.125 = random guessing); robust aggregation shrugs the attack off and keeps full accuracy. Essential for real hospital consortia.
Efficiency

Cutting the network cost 50× (communication)

Federated learning’s real-world bottleneck is bandwidth — every round each hospital uploads the whole model (~45 MB). We send only the largest 1% of update values and skip the rest.

Update sentUpload size / roundCompressionBalanced accuracy
Full model (100%)44.7 MB1×0.205
Top 10% of values8.9 MB5×0.197
Top 1% of values0.89 MB50×0.202
What we added & why
Top-k sparsification: send only the values that matter. New here, and it pairs perfectly with the edge/Raspberry-Pi story below (a weak device on a slow link).
Why it matters
50× smaller uploads for essentially the same accuracy (0.202 vs 0.205). Makes FL practical for bandwidth-limited or edge hospitals.
Trust

Looking inside the model (Explainability)

For a clinical model, we should check where it looks. Grad-CAM highlights the image regions that drove each decision.

An honest limitation we surfaced
On our small, compute-limited model the attention is diffuse and sometimes lands on image borders/artifacts rather than the lesion — a known “shortcut” in dermoscopy AI. We report this openly: it’s exactly the kind of problem explainability is meant to catch, and it would gate a real deployment.
Why it matters
Running the check — and being honest about what it revealed — is the responsible-AI point. A production model would need stronger, artifact-free attention before clinical use.
Real hardware

From simulation to a real device: the Raspberry Pi

Everything above is a simulation on one computer. To prove it’s genuinely distributed, we ran the system across two real machines: a laptop as the coordinator and a Raspberry Pi 5 as a real ‘edge hospital’, talking over the network.

What we added & why
A real client–server deployment (Flower/gRPC): the Pi trains on its own data and sends only weights. It turns “federated” from a claim into a demonstrated fact on hardware.
Why it matters
It shows an ~€80 device can join training with no data leaving it — and it exposes the straggler problem (the whole round waits for the slowest device), a real systems insight the simulation can’t show. This is why the 50× compression above matters for edge devices.
Reference

Glossary — every term in plain words

Quick definitions so nothing on this page needs outside explanation.

Federated Learning (FL)
Training one shared model across many data owners by exchanging model updates, not raw data.
Cross-silo
The FL setting with a few, persistent, data-rich clients — here, hospitals.
Non-IID
The clients’ data is not identically distributed — each hospital has a different mix. This makes FL harder.
Balanced accuracy
Average accuracy across all 8 classes (so rare diseases count equally). Random guessing = 0.125.
Client drift
How far the hospitals’ locally-trained models pull apart each round; large drift can destabilise the average.
Differential Privacy (DP) / ε
A math guarantee that no single patient noticeably affects the model; ε is the privacy strength (smaller = more private).
Membership inference
An attack that guesses whether a specific person was in the training data. AUC 0.5 = attacker learns nothing.
Secure aggregation
A scheme where the server can add up hospital updates but cannot read any single one.
Poisoning / Byzantine
A malicious client sending bad updates to sabotage the model.
Robust aggregation (Krum, median, trimmed-mean)
Ways to combine updates that ignore extreme/outlier (malicious) ones.
Sparsification (top-k)
Sending only the largest few percent of update values to save bandwidth.
Straggler
The slowest device in a synchronous round; everyone waits for it.
Grad-CAM
A method that highlights which image regions drove a model’s decision.
